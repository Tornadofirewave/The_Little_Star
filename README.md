The core gameplay loop is that the player activates various mechanisms that will help them reach a platform that serves as an end of a level. They will have various tools that will assist them in doing so, currently being limited to responsive movement and a star projectile that also activates mechanisms. Players can interact with two types of bubbles, orange which give a simple launch upwards, and green which also grants a launch upwards but are required to interact with at least once to open the door to the next level. The gameplay and content of this version of the game heavily differs from the original plan found within my vertical slice, as I found the gameplay loop of the original vertical slice to be plain and uninteresting. The vision of the game changed dramatically once I implemented the bubble launch prefab and found the sensation of staying in the air while moving around to be much more fun fundamentally. The current gameplay and content illustrate to the player what the full game would look like by demonstrating the intricacies and diverse player strategy and skill that can be gained through the challenging platforming mechanics. While some players would be capable of clearing the game while not using any of the given tools (the star), it is dramatically easier and more rewarding for the players to use the new abilities they're granted with and the environment around them to traverse further up the levels. The aesthetics of the original vertical slice is similar to where the player is in space, defined by the subtle "space" hues of the tiles changing between purple, red, and blue, alongside the ambient white particles that serve as miniature stars.

The rendering effect I used was manipulating the color values of a tilemap to phase through the three different hues of dark purple, red, and blue.

I primarily used UV, lerp, multiply, add, sine, and remap nodes to achieve this effect. The attached screenshot displays a slightly more ambitious attempt at getting a texture to play on the tiles as well, however attempts to do so led to unsatisfactory results at best so that idea was cut. First, we took a time node and a color speed float and multiplied them together. This allowed us to control the pace of which the colors would phase. We took the output of that multiply node and attached it to separate inputs, one that would control the pace of how long each color should last utilizing an add and sine node, applying that to a remap node. The other connection utilizes a sine and remap node as well, however this is where we connected two of the base colors to a lerp node that also takes in a texture input taken by the remap node. We wanted three colors, but the lerp node only accepted two, so we'll connect it to another lerp node that takes the two color lerp as the first value, the third color as the second, and lastly connect the first remap node to the texture to get all three colors phasing equally. I wanted to get a texture working so we connected it to another lerp node with the texture being applied, but ignoring that since that wasn't implemented, I connected it back to the fragment node's base color so the color would change properly.

I first identified what the core "toy" of my game would be, which would be player movement. I was very inspired by Hollow Knight's very sensitive and responsive movement system, so I went ahead and created my own player movement logic rather than relying on the existing rigidbody 2D gravity systems. I wanted to ensure that players felt like they had absolute control over the player. Once I went through the specifics of gravity gates, horizontal velocities, apex checks, etc. I was satisfied with the current player movement. Once it was fun walking and jumping around without anything else, I looked into the other mechanics I wanted to implement. I didn't focus on art for now, simply using white boxes and a simple player sprite I whipped up. I created a "button" and door system, where once all the buttons in a room were pressed, a door would open. It was a simple goal and I was able to test what it would feel like trying to get the buttons. However, this was unsatisfactory and I resulted in playing around with other possible options during this playtesting phase. Eventually, I found that applying vertical velocity to the player made it feel much more high energy which I believe coincided well with the player movement being so responsive. From there, I made a plan to implement platforms that would serve as level progression, utilize the same door and button systems to create a room progression, and from there begin polishing with proper tiles, sfx, vfx, etc. My main process for a larger project was simply to make sure that the core toy was fun first, then begin adding garnishes in the form of interesting ways to re-frame that toy and test understanding, while adding some player responsiveness.

While I appreciated the bubble diagram breakdowns as we learned them, I found my way of designing to be a lot more free-form. I believe I could use it if I needed to concept the core toy, but from there I feel like a lot of decisions can be made just from how it feels. From there, once a surface level of polish is created, begin thorough playtesting and list the possible improvements and implement those. I found the task of laying out every aspect that will exist in a bubble diagram to be tedious, as I don't know how the game will feel by simply looking at a diagram. Once I have the toy in front of me, and I think it's fun, I can brainstorm ways to make it even more fun. Simply, the idea of a fun toy doesn't mean the toy will be fun until it's in front of you and you can see if it works or not.

The process of breaking down a large project into smaller steps greatly aids the understanding of the scope of a project. I have made a few games now, and understanding the baseline mechanics is what I always prioritize. If I am able to visualize how those mechanics will be programmed just through steps and build that mental understanding, I have a foundation of udnerstanding of what the game will look like rather than getting bogged down by "cool ideas." My approach allows me to ensure that I can understand how something will be laid out, and once that's achieved, I re-enter the scoping process to expand it if the idea proves successful. I keep my scope small, nearly miniscule, as I don't know what adjustments I have to make in order to make a game fun, and I believe only playtesting and reveal that.

The plan I've described has heavily related to my experience in my vertical slice project, and most of my prototyping process. Things went alright, playtesters had positive feedback regarding the movement of the player character and that was pretty fundamental to me that moving around was not a chore. Players enjoyed the challenge that it was to learn how to progress and take their understandings and stretch them. The original prototype was fairly boring, but through receiving playtesting feedback I was able to understand their desires from the, at the time, flat game and morph it into what it is now. I quite like my approach of hyper-focusing on the core gameplay, what the player will be doing for the majority of the game (moving, shooting, etc.) and ensuring that it is a baseline level of fun.
